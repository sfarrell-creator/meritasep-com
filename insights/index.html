From no pipeline to five qualified conversations a month

A founder brought us in because sales lived entirely in his head. No process, no team, no CRM anyone trusted. Every new customer came from his own network, and that network was running out.

We didn't start by hiring salespeople. We started by writing down what actually happened when a deal closed: who the buyer was, what made them call back, and what question always came up right before they said yes.

From there we built the whole outreach engine:

  • A defined target list instead of "anyone who might buy"
  • A short sequence of touches, written once and reused every week
  • One place to track every conversation, so nothing depended on the founder's memory
  • A weekly number everyone could see: qualified conversations held

Within a few months the company went from no real pipeline to about five qualified conversations a month. The process stuck after we stepped back. A couple of years later, the company sold.

A sales process isn't a team. It's a set of decisions written down so they stop depending on one person.

Operations

It's not an effort problem. Nobody owns it.

The same pattern shows up in almost every founder-led company we walk into. People are working hard. Things still fall through. When you trace it back, it's rarely effort. It's that nobody clearly owns the job.

The usual suspects:

  • Collections. Past-due invoices get chased when someone remembers, usually by calling and asking nicely. No aging review, no escalation steps.
  • Bill pay. Vendors get paid late or twice because three people half-own it.
  • Debt. Lines, loans and equipment notes spread across banks with no single view of balances, rates or due dates.
  • Marketing. Money goes out every month for years with no one able to say what it produces.

The fix is less dramatic than people expect. Put one name next to each function. Give that person one number to watch and a weekly slot to report it. Most of these problems shrink the first month someone is accountable for them.

If two people own it, nobody owns it.

What a buyer checks first, even if you're never selling

Love them or hate them, private equity firms are very good at one thing: they know every number in a business, and they measure each one against the industry. When we look at a company the way a buyer would, we look at six areas:

  • Market position and growth story. Why customers pick you, and why that lasts.
  • Quality of earnings. Whether the profit on paper is the profit that's really there.
  • Unit economics and pricing. Which customers, jobs or products actually make money.
  • Tax exposure and entity structure. What an audit or a diligence team would find.
  • Legal, governance and people. Whether the company runs without the founder in every decision.
  • Systems and operational discipline. Whether the numbers show up on time, every month, without heroics.

Most owners we meet aren't selling anytime soon. That's the point. A business that would hold up to a buyer is also easier to run, easier to finance, and worth more the day you do decide.

Run it like someone is about to buy it, and you won't have to scramble when someone does.
